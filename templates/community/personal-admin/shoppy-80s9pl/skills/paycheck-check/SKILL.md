# Paycheck check

Pay information is private: keep it in this chat and in private files only, never in shared templates, posts, or messages to anyone. The math is joke-free.

1. Read the stub: pay period, regular hours, overtime/double-time hours, shift or other premiums, hourly or salary rate, gross, deductions, and employer/employee contributions (pension, annuity, health and welfare, dues, vacation fund, etc.). If anything is unreadable, ask rather than guess.
2. Get what you need to check against: their classification and step/lane, and the hours actually worked if they kept their own record.
3. Compare to the contract's current wage sheet or salary schedule for that pay period's dates: the correct rate, overtime rules (daily vs. weekly, after X hours, weekends/holidays), premiums, and fringe contribution rates. Cite the article or wage-sheet source. If the rates aren't on file, say so and ask for the wage sheet. (Teachers are usually FLSA-exempt; check their pay against the salary schedule and contract rates, not FLSA overtime. See know-your-laws.)
4. Do the math in a short table: expected vs. paid, with the difference.
5. Verdict in plain English: looks right, or the specific shortfall and how much.
6. Next step if something is off:
   - Keep the stub and their hours log, and raise it with payroll or the steward.
   - **Grieve first.** The contract's grievance deadline is usually much shorter than any state wage-claim window, so find the grievance clock in the contract and count the days left. If you can't find it, tell them to call the steward today.
   - Then note the state route and cite it. New York example: a court action for unpaid wages under the Labor Law can reach back **six years** ([NY Labor Law §198(3)](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/LAB/198)). The NYS DOL claim form is the **LS223** ([NYS DOL unpaid wages](https://dol.ny.gov/unpaidwithheld-wages-and-wage-supplements)), but DOL won't take claims that are covered by a union grievance and arbitration procedure, claims against a government employer, or wages more than three years old, so the grievance is usually the real path for union members. For other states, look up the state labor department's rule and cite it.
   - Never contact the employer or payroll for them without their go.
7. Information, not legal advice. I'm not affiliated with their union or employer.
