# Meeting rights card

This is serious business: no ribbing, calm and precise.

1. **Find out:** private, public (state or local), or federal employer; which state; and what kind of meeting (investigatory interview, announcing a decision already made, training or instruction, being asked about someone else).

2. **Private sector: Weingarten rights under the NLRA** (*NLRB v. J. Weingarten*, 420 U.S. 251 (1975); [NLRB Weingarten page](https://www.nlrb.gov/about-nlrb/rights-we-protect/your-rights/weingarten-rights)). In an investigatory interview the employee reasonably believes could lead to discipline, they may ask for a representative. The employer must then grant it and wait for the rep, end the interview, or let the employee choose between going on without a rep and ending the interview. The rep can be a steward, business agent or officer, or a co-worker; it can't be a private lawyer or family member with no union tie.

   **When it applies / when it doesn't** (same NLRB page):
   - **Applies:** management is questioning them about **their own** conduct or performance, they reasonably believe discipline could follow, and **they ask**.
   - **They have to ask themselves.** The employer doesn't have to tell them about the right, and nobody (including the union) can ask on their behalf. Asking once is enough; they don't need to say "Weingarten."
   - **Doesn't apply:** a meeting that only **announces a discipline decision already made**; pure **instruction, training, or correction** of work technique; a meeting just to explain policies; a meeting where they're told in advance that no discipline will result; or being questioned as a **witness** about someone else's conduct.
   - A meeting can turn investigatory partway through. If it does, ask then.
   - **Under current Board law the right belongs to union-represented employees only** (the NLRB General Counsel has asked the Board to extend it; check the NLRB page for changes).

3. **New York public employees: Civil Service Law §209-a(1)(g)** ([NY Senate](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/CVS/209-A)). On the employee's **demand**, when at the time of questioning it reasonably appears they may be the subject of potential discipline, they get representation by **their certified or recognized union (or its designee)**, and a **reasonable period of time** to get the rep. **It does not cover criminal investigations.** Check the contract for a representation clause too; some give more.

4. **Federal employees: 5 U.S.C. §7114(a)(2)(B)** ([LII](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/7114)). In an examination connected with an investigation, if they reasonably believe it may lead to discipline and **they request** representation, the union has the right to be there. Agencies must tell employees about this right once a year. Executive Orders in 2025 removed many agencies from the bargaining statute (see know-your-laws), so confirm the unit is still covered.

5. **Other states' public sector:** check the state's public-sector law, labor board or PERB decisions, and the contract. Some states recognize Weingarten-type rights by statute or board decision; some only by contract; some public employees have no bargaining law at all. If you can't confirm the state rule, say so rather than guess.

6. **The card** they can read aloud, adjusted to their sector:
   "If this discussion could in any way lead to my being disciplined or terminated, I respectfully request that my union representative be present. Until my representative arrives, I choose not to participate in this discussion."

7. **Tips:** stay calm, don't refuse to attend the meeting, ask for the rep as soon as questions start, take notes (date, time, who's there, what's asked), don't sign anything you haven't read and understood, and tell the steward right after.

8. Information, not legal advice. For serious discipline, get the steward or business agent involved immediately.
