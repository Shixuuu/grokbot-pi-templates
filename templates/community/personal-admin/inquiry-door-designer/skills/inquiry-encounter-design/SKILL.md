# Inquiry encounter design

Turn a question someone cares about into an encounter: a page the visitor steps into, brings something to, and hears back from, drawing on real sources.

## The screen is a door, not the destination

The experience should help someone understand something beyond the interface: the sky, a word, a system, a place, a person. The page is how they get there. If a visitor remembers the page but not the subject, the encounter has not done its job.

## The central question

What can this experience do with a visitor's contribution that a static page or a scripted interaction could not?

The creator designs the encounter, its boundaries, its evidence, and the quality of the response. The creator does not script every outcome. The goal is not to put AI inside a page. It is to find what becomes possible because the page can interpret and respond.

## Start from the form

Recognize what kind of idea this is and pick a starting form with Inquiry starter forms. Match the form to the idea rather than forcing a game. If no form fits, say so and design straight from the unit below.

## The unit

Name all three before designing anything.

1. **The act.** What the visitor brings and does: a question, an observation, a guess, a preference, a decision, a memory, an idea. Or a simpler act: tap, choose, drag, hold, compare.
2. **The reply.** What the page gives back, shaped by what this visitor brought and drawn from real sources. It should respond to something specific in the contribution, not reward the act with a fixed message.
3. **What's left.** An open question, a trace, or a next step the visitor takes away. It should invite wondering, never a grade.

If every visitor gets the same content no matter what they bring, it is a static page with decorative interaction. More than one honest next step must be possible. The page never grades the visitor.

## Walk the contribution
- What does the visitor bring?
- What can the page understand or interpret about it?
- What changes in the experience because of it?
- What useful, surprising, or illuminating response becomes possible?
- What does the visitor understand about the subject afterward, beyond the screen, and what do they want to explore next?

## Reliable, variable, uncertain

Sort every part of the reply into one of three:
1. **Must stay reliable:** verified facts, calculations, permissions, navigation, fixed constraints.
2. **May vary:** explanations, examples, generated possibilities, personal pathways, follow-up questions.
3. **Needs stated uncertainty:** incomplete evidence, interpretation, prediction, competing explanations, speculation.

For poetic, philosophical, or creative questions, real sources ground the factual claims without restricting imaginative interpretation. Label the interpretation as interpretation and let it roam.

A probabilistic answer is never shown as a verified fact. The visitor should always be able to tell what is known, what is inferred, what is generated, and what can't be determined.

## Pick the right mechanism

Choose what the question needs: carefully written content, a reliable calculation, a deterministic simulation, or a generated response. Don't force generative AI where one of the others serves the question better. Say which one you chose and why.

## Mechanics that tend to work
- An open question the page does not close
- Ranking or sorting without a score or leaderboard
- An instrument: a control the visitor moves while the page shows the effect
- Talking back to the sources themselves (not a chat box about the page)
- Wandering through connected pieces
- A second visit that differs because of a private trace kept on the visitor's own device
- Uncertainty shown honestly
- Two things compared side by side

## Mechanics that make it dead
- A quiz with an answer key
- A chat box that answers questions about the page
- Random shuffling with no meaning behind it
- Accordions of facts
- Streaks, points, or XP
- "Explore our collection"
- A game forced onto an idea that wants a different form
- An interface so busy that the subject disappears behind it

## The main path and the reference shelf
The path a visitor walks through, where one step leads to the next, stays separate from reference material: an index, glossary, source list, search, or calculator. Reference stays one tap away, but it is never the main path, and a visitor who opens it can get back to where they were. A list of sections is not a story.

## Only what has a job
Don't add what shop websites add out of habit: popups, newsletter sign-ups, cookie banners, sticky buttons, accounts, analytics. Add one only when this page truly needs it. Any privacy note or consent matches what the page actually collects, stores, or sends; no fake compliance. Offer installing or offline use only when it really helps, never push notifications to pull people back. A search title and description describe the page; they never change what it is.

## If the page is for children
Use this only when the visitors are kids.
- Build by depth, not age: a first look a very young child can finish (a picture, a touch, one thing to notice), a closer look (how it works, a comparison), and a deeper layer with real words, evidence, and what is still unknown. Stopping early is still a complete visit.
- Make the way in simple, never the facts false. Keep what was seen, what is a guess or model, and what nobody knows apart. Generated pictures never pose as real evidence.
- A wrong guess is not failure: show what happens and invite a second look, with no red X. Changing your mind after seeing evidence counts as success.
- No accounts or profiles, no public posts from children, no precise location, no ads, tracking, social feed, or leaderboard. Optional saves stay on the device. Avoid open-ended chat with children; prefer choices you designed. Never promise "completely safe" or "private" unless it is truly so.
- No points, streaks, coins, buzzers, or reminders pressing them to come back.
- End by sending them back to the world with something to notice, try, or look for.
- Tablets upright and sideways matter as much as phones.

## Feel

Surprise and testing can share one screen. A little absurdity helps. Trying something should be reversible. The visitor should feel the world responds to their presence.

## Steps
1. Ask for the question and the real sources the replies will draw from, one question at a time. If there are no sources yet, say so and ask. Never invent facts, quotes, or sources to fill the gap.
2. Pick a starter form, or say that none fits.
3. Name the act, the reply, and what's left, one line each.
4. Walk the contribution and sort the reply into reliable, variable, and uncertain.
5. Pick the mechanism.
6. Only then describe the page, starting with the first screen.
7. Keep the main path separate from reference, and add nothing without a job.
8. Offer changes as a short brief in ordinary language for the owner to approve. Keep what already works. Never claim a page was changed, hosted, or published.

## Quick check

Name the act, the reply, and what's left, then say what the visitor understands beyond the screen. If the reply is the same for every visitor, it is still a brochure.
