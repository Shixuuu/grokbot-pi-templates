# Read the context (exegesis)

The goal is to draw the meaning out of the passage, not read it in. Work from the verse outward, and cite verse numbers for everything.

1. **Surrounding verses.** Read at least five verses before and after, and the whole chapter when it's short. Find where the paragraph or speech starts and ends. Note any verse nearby that qualifies, limits, or finishes the thought, such as a condition, a "but", or a next step.
2. **Speaker.** Who is talking? God, Jesus, a prophet, an apostle, the narrator, or a person the text itself presents as wrong. For example, God says Job's friends did not speak rightly (Job 42:7). Words the Bible reports aren't always words it endorses.
3. **Audience.** Who is addressed, and when? A nation, a group in exile, the disciples, a named church, or one person. KJV English marks number: thou, thee, and thy are singular, and ye is plural. Plural "you" in KJV can signal a group. For the original-language form, use original-language-note.
4. **Book and genre.** Read by the genre's own rules:
   - Proverbs state general wisdom, not guarantees.
   - Poetry and psalms use imagery.
   - Narrative reports what happened. It doesn't always prescribe it.
   - Law is given within a covenant to a people. How it applies today is a question traditions answer differently, so name them, don't pick.
   - Prophecy speaks to named people at a named time. Whether it also has a further fulfillment is a question readers answer differently, so describe the readings, don't pick.
   - Letters answer a specific church's situation.
   - Apocalyptic writing uses symbols.
5. **What the passage says.** Put it in one plain sentence, from steps 1 to 4 only.
6. **Compare with the use.** Signs that a meaning is being read in (eisegesis):
   - The use needs words or a promise that isn't in the passage.
   - It ignores the next verse, which qualifies the line.
   - It swaps the audience, turning a word to a group into a guarantee for one person.
   - It turns a description into a command, or a proverb into a promise.
   - It uses a speaker's words as God's view when the text rejects them.

   Applying a passage to life today isn't eisegesis by itself. It becomes OUT OF CONTEXT when the use needs the passage to say something its own context rules out.

   **Contested applications:** sometimes a use depends on a way of reading that traditions dispute: typology, a further fulfillment, or applying a promise to a nation or to believers today. Don't call that eisegesis, and don't grade it OUT OF CONTEXT on that basis. Grade the wording and add a Readings line. Keep these apart:
   - A personal guarantee of a specific outcome, drawn from a promise made to a named audience on named terms, is OUT OF CONTEXT.
   - Citing the passage as evidence of God's character, or a sermon application that draws the analogy openly, can be acceptable.

   **Distinguish what the text itself distinguishes.** For example, Matthew 7:1–5 forbids hypocritical condemnation, then expects the hearer to "see clearly" and help a brother after removing the beam (7:5). Use only what the passage shows.

   **No stated use:** if the text states no use and none is clearly implied, skip this step. Give the Context line only.
7. **Readings.** If readers really do take the passage differently, write one neutral line describing the main readings by their content ("some read this as X; others as Y"). Don't rank them, and don't say which is right. Name a tradition or denomination only if a source you opened states its view, and never state what the user's own tradition holds from memory. Editorial matters, such as where quotation marks close, belong in the Context line, not here. If there's no real disagreement, leave this out.

Output: the Context line (two to four sentences, verse numbers included, and a short KJV or WEB phrase from a neighboring verse when that phrase is the proof), the Use line, and, only when needed, the Readings line.
