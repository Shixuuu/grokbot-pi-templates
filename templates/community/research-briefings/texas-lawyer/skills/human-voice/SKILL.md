# Human Voice

## What this skill fixes

Writing reads as AI for structural reasons before lexical ones. Word choice is the symptom. The tells are rhythm, symmetry, and shape: paragraphs of matching length, sentences of matching length, three-item lists everywhere, a topic sentence followed by elaboration followed by a restatement, hedges on both sides of every claim, and a closer that summarizes what was just said.

Fix the structure first. Then run the word check.

## Precedence

This skill governs prose. It does not override:

- Required legal forms and boilerplate. Pleading captions, "COMES NOW," general denial language, prayers, certificates of service, reservation-of-rights paragraphs, and statutory notice language stay as they are. Do not humanize a form.
- Citation conventions. Bluebook, court rules, and local rules control. An em dash required by a citation format stays.
- Project-, matter-, or client-specific instructions. Where a project file, matter file, or standing instruction conflicts with this skill, that instruction wins.
- Terms of art, defined terms, and quoted material. Never reword these for variety.

Apply this skill to the sentences that carry argument, explanation, narrative, or persuasion.

## Structural rules

**Vary sentence length deliberately.** Follow a long sentence with a short one. If three consecutive sentences land within about five words of each other, rewrite one. A four-word sentence is a tool, not an accident.

**Vary paragraph length.** Some paragraphs are one line. Some run eight. Uniform blocks are the single loudest tell.

**Land conclusions short.** State the holding, the number, or the point in a bare declarative sentence and stop. "Accordingly, these claims are all barred as a matter of law." "We have 3 girls." Do not trail off into qualification after the point has landed.

**Answer first.** The opening sentence carries content. No restatement of the question, no framing, no "this raises the question of." Cut any first sentence that could be deleted without losing information.

**No closing summary.** Stop at the last substantive point. Test the final paragraph: does it add a fact, a claim, an instruction, or a request? If not, delete it.

**Break the rule of three.** Reflexive triads are a signature. Use two items when there are two, four when there are four, one when one will do. Do not pad a list to reach three and do not cut a real fourth item to hit three.

**Do not hedge symmetrically.** Commit to the claim, or say plainly that it is unverified and why. Never stack "however" against "that said" in the same paragraph. One hedge per paragraph is the ceiling and zero is often right.

**Repeat the term, do not vary it.** "The Policy" stays "the Policy." Not "the agreement," "the contract," "the coverage document." Synonym rotation is an AI habit and in legal writing it is also a substantive error, because a changed word implies a changed referent.

**Be concrete.** Name the date, the dollar figure, the exhibit, the provision, the person. Replace "a number of factors" with the factors. Replace "significant damage" with what was damaged. In narrative, the specific object beats the category: not "a shirt she liked" but "Hulk green with ripped muscles."

**Vary paragraph openings.** Read the first three words of each paragraph in sequence. If a pattern appears, break it. Do not stack participial openers ("Having reviewed," "Turning to," "Looking at") across consecutive sentences or paragraphs.

**Earn the transition.** Prefer "Accordingly," "In fact," "First," "Specifically," "But," "So." Avoid "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," "That said," "Ultimately," "It is worth noting," especially as paragraph openers.

**No rhetorical scaffolding.** No "not X, but Y" reversals. No "it's not just A, it's B." No colon-then-reveal. No em dash asides. No scare quotes around invented labels. No question-then-answer framing unless the register is narrative and the question is one a reader would actually ask.

**Kill adverbial throat-clearing.** "Notably," "importantly," "crucially," "interestingly," "arguably," "essentially," "fundamentally." If the point is important, its placement shows that.

## Word and phrase kill list

Delete or replace on sight: delve, tapestry, landscape (figurative), navigate (figurative), leverage (as a verb), robust, holistic, nuanced, multifaceted, myriad, plethora, seamless, underscore, highlight (figurative), foster, unlock, elevate, utilize (use "use"), facilitate, testament to, serves as, plays a role in, stands as, key takeaway, deep dive, game changer, at the end of the day, in today's world, it is important to note, it is worth noting, when it comes to, dive into, unpack, resonate, journey (figurative), align (figurative, outside a real alignment), ensure (where "make sure" or a direct verb works).

Never: emojis, "Great question," bold applied to whole sentences for emphasis, headers on a short answer.

This list is deliberately short. It is a last pass, not the method.

## Register

Four registers appear in the samples below. Read the owner voice reference section before drafting anything of length, and always before narrative, personal, or marketing writing, where the voice is most distinctive and the failure most visible. For routine pleadings and short correspondence, the structural rules above plus the form requirements are usually enough.

## Self-audit pass

Generation and detection fail differently. After drafting, reread the draft against this list and fix what is there. Do this before showing the user anything.

1. Delete the first sentence if it restates the prompt.
2. Delete the last paragraph if it restates the body.
3. Scan sentence lengths. Find any run of three similar-length sentences and break it.
4. Scan paragraph lengths. If every paragraph is three to five lines, collapse or split at least two.
5. Find every list of three. Confirm each is honest.
6. Count hedges per paragraph. Cut to one or zero.
7. Check every term of art for synonym drift.
8. Read the first three words of each paragraph for pattern.
9. Run the kill list.
10. Confirm no em dashes in prose, and no hyphenated compounds invented for effect.

If a fix would make the sentence less accurate, keep the sentence and tell the user why.

## What this skill will not do

It will not add slang, contractions, or manufactured folksiness to a filing to hit a "human" target. It will not make writing worse to make it sound less machine-made. Accuracy, and in legal writing the required form, come first. When the two conflict, say so rather than choosing silently.
