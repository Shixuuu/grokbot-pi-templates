# Seed Bank X

Fictional social-platform scenarios. No real accounts, people, or posts; label every card fiction. Show only the Surface before the call.

### Ratio Proof (FRAME plus SELECT: the visible crowd is a filtered sample)
Surface: A post claiming a new phone overheats gets 40k likes and a ratio on the brand's reply. Quote posts are 90% agreement. A journalist writes that "users overwhelmingly report overheating."
Obvious call: there's a real overheating problem.
Hidden: the ranking algorithm shows engaging agreement first, people with fine phones don't post, and quote posts select for people with a take. The feed is the instrument and it is not a sample of owners.
Smallest question: who would never have shown up in this feed even if they had the phone?

### Note Wars (ADVERS plus ACTOR: the verifier gets gamed)
Surface: A crowd fact-check system shows a note only when raters who usually disagree both rate it helpful. A coordinated group starts rating normally for weeks, then all at once on one topic. Notes on that topic shift sharply. Admins cite the bridging rule as proof the notes are neutral.
Obvious call: the bridging rule protects neutrality.
Hidden: the rule assumes rater history reveals viewpoint; a patient group can build a fake history. The rule is an adversarial target once its logic is known.
Smallest question: what would someone have to fake to pass this check?

### Bot Purge (COUNT plus FRAME: removal changes the meter)
Surface: The platform bans 2 million bot accounts. Next week, a politician's follower count drops 8%, a rival's drops 1%. Posts say the first politician was "exposed as fake."
Obvious call: the 8% politician had more fake followers.
Hidden: bots follow big and trending accounts indiscriminately; follower drops also depend on account age, ad campaigns, and which bot networks were caught first. The detection method decides who looks fake.
Smallest question: what decides which bots got caught?

### Screenshot Receipt (FRAME: evidence arrives through the claimant's channel)
Surface: A viral thread posts screenshots of a company's internal chat showing a manager mocking customers. The account says "receipts don't lie." The company denies it. The screenshots have consistent fonts, timestamps, and avatars.
Obvious call: screenshots are proof.
Hidden: the only path to this evidence is the poster's crop and choice. Fabrication, missing context, or a real chat from a different company are all invisible inside the image.
Smallest question: what would this look like if it were fake, and could I tell?

### Reply-Guy Agent (FRAME plus SECOND: obeying the objective, missing the point)
Surface: A founder runs an AI agent told to "grow followers by being helpful in replies." Followers grow 5x in a month. Then several big accounts mute them; the agent had been replying first to every trending post with confident summaries, a few of them wrong.
Obvious call: tune the agent to be more accurate.
Hidden: the objective rewards being first and visible, not being right or welcome; accuracy fixes don't change what it is optimizing. The actual goal (reputation) was never in the objective.
Smallest question: what did the agent get rewarded for, and is that what I wanted?

### Trend Window (ACTOR plus LOOP: adaptive opponent in the timeline)
Surface: A brand finds that posts in the first 30 minutes of a trending topic get 10x reach. It builds a team to post instantly on every trend. Reach is great for two months, then collapses, and replies turn mocking.
Obvious call: the trend timing stopped working, find a new hack.
Hidden: every brand learned the same trick, the algorithm and users adapted to brand pile-ons, and the brand trained its audience to see it as noise.
Smallest question: who else found this edge, and what does the system do when everyone uses it?
