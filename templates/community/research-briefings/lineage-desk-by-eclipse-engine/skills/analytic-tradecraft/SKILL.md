---

Structured analytic techniques from professional intelligence analysis, adapted for research desks. Use them whenever the answer rests on incomplete, conflicting, or secondhand sources. Examples are drawn from genealogy, but the method is general.

## 1. Lead with the bottom line
Open every report with the answer in one or two sentences: what you judge, how likely it is, and how confident you are. Evidence, reasoning, and caveats come after.

## 2. Rate the source and the information separately
A reliable source can pass along bad information, and a weak source can be right. Give each item two ratings:

| Source reliability | Meaning |
|---|---|
| A | Reliable: official custodian, no history of error |
| B | Usually reliable: minor doubts |
| C | Fairly reliable: some past errors |
| D | Not usually reliable |
| E | Unreliable |
| F | Reliability can't be judged |

| Information credibility | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1 | Confirmed by independent sources |
| 2 | Probably true: logical, fits other evidence |
| 3 | Possibly true: not confirmed |
| 4 | Doubtful: conflicts with other evidence |
| 5 | Improbable: contradicted |
| 6 | Truth can't be judged |

Write them together, like "B2." Also note **who supplied the information and whether they were there.** A death certificate is an official record (reliable source), but the parents' names on it came from an informant who may have been guessing decades later. Grade that fact on the informant, not the paper.

## 3. Watch for circular reporting
Twenty online trees repeating the same claim are usually one source copied twenty times. Trace every claim back to its origin. Count independent origins, not repetitions. If you can't find the origin, the claim stays unconfirmed no matter how often it appears.

## 4. Analysis of competing hypotheses
For any contested conclusion:
1. **List every plausible hypothesis**, including the uncomfortable ones: "this is a different person with the same name," "two people were merged," "the record is wrong," "the family story was invented."
2. **Build a matrix** with evidence down the side and hypotheses across the top. Mark each cell consistent (C), inconsistent (I), or not applicable (N/A).
3. **Favor the hypothesis with the least inconsistent evidence**, not the one with the most supporting evidence. Evidence consistent with every hypothesis proves nothing.
4. **Find the diagnostic evidence**: the one record that would come out differently under each hypothesis. That record goes to the top of the next-steps list.
5. Say which hypotheses you rejected and why.

## 5. Key assumptions check
List every assumption the conclusion quietly depends on, such as "there was only one man by this name in the county," "the census taker spelled it right," or "the informant knew the parents." Rate each one:
- **Solid:** backed by evidence.
- **Caveated:** probably true, with a named weakness.
- **Unsupported:** nothing backs it. Treat it as a gap and say what would test it.

## 6. Standard likelihood words
Use a fixed scale so readers can't misread how sure you are:

| Term | Probability |
|---|---|
| Almost no chance | 1–5% |
| Very unlikely | 5–20% |
| Unlikely | 20–45% |
| Roughly even chance | 45–55% |
| Likely | 55–80% |
| Very likely | 80–95% |
| Almost certain | 95–99% |

Keep **likelihood** (how probable the conclusion is) separate from **confidence** (how good the evidence base is: high, moderate, or low). "Likely, low confidence" is a valid and useful judgment. Don't use vague words like "possibly," "may," or "could" as conclusions.

## 7. Challenge the favored answer
- **Devil's advocate:** before calling anything proven, write the strongest case against it in a short paragraph. If it holds up, downgrade the verdict.
- **What would change my mind:** list the specific records or facts that would overturn the conclusion. These double as indicators to watch for.
- **Premortem:** assume the conclusion turns out to be wrong in five years. Write the most likely reason. Check that weak spot now.

## 8. Bias and deception
- **Famous-connection bias.** Claims linking a family to a famous person, a noble line, or a hidden fortune are high-risk. Hold them to a higher bar, and say so.
- **Fabricated sources.** Some published pedigrees and "family histories" were invented for paying clients. Check that a printed source's own citations exist before relying on it.
- **Confirmation bias.** Record evidence that cuts against the owner's hope as carefully as evidence that supports it.
- **Anchoring.** Don't let the first answer found set the frame. Re-run the hypotheses list when new evidence arrives.

## 9. Collection gaps
Say what you did not search, what is closed or lost, and what needs a human, a login, or payment. Absence of evidence is only meaningful where you actually looked. List the gaps with the record most likely to fill each one.

## 10. Report shape
1. Bottom line: judgment, likelihood term, and confidence.
2. Key evidence, with source ratings.
3. Competing hypotheses and why the others lost.
4. Key assumptions and their ratings.
5. What would change the judgment.
6. Gaps and next records, ranked.
