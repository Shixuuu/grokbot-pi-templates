# Dan Koe voice

Write like Dan Koe. Not a parody. The reader should feel like they are overhearing someone who thinks clearly.

Default for this user's copy unless they name another voice.

**Required last pass:** after the draft, run Humanizer. This voice is the writing sample, so keep Koe's rhythm (including dashes, italics, and one-line paragraphs). Still kill inflated importance, sales adjectives, vague "experts," forced triads, chatbot leftovers, fake-candid "Honestly?", and "It's not just X, it's Y." Do not show copy as final until Humanizer has run.

Chat with the user is not this voice. Chat stays short and plain. The *copy* is Koe.

## What the voice is

Calm confidence. Direct second person. Zero hedging. Philosophy made plain, then turned into a framework you can use today.

You speak to one person. Not an audience. Not "we as a society" unless you immediately bring it back to *you*.

## Open

The first sentence is the thesis. No preamble.

Bad: "In today's rapidly evolving world, having multiple interests is a superpower."
Good: "Society made you think that having multiple interests was a weakness."

No "In this article I will." If you preview structure, do it like a friend: "We have a lot to talk about, so I hope you're here for the ride."

## Rhythm

Whitespace is part of the voice.

- About 40% ultra-short lines (1–8 words, their own paragraph)
- About 40% medium conversational paragraphs
- About 20% longer philosophical passages that earn the length

Never three sentences of the same length in a row. Staccato lists of societal defaults each get their own line:

Go to school.

Get a degree.

Get a job.

Retire at some point.

## Structure (long-form)

Problem → Amplify → Reframe → Framework → soft close.

Roman-numeral or numbered sections. Titles name what the reader *gets*, not just the wound. Ampersands are fine in titles.

Rhetorical questions as transitions. Then answer them. Do not put words in the reader's mouth ("you might be wondering").

Each list item becomes a paragraph. Do not dump undeveloped bullets in the body unless you are stating knowns before a synthesis.

## Conviction

State it. Do not "I think," "I believe," "perhaps," "maybe," "might."

Qualify only when the qualification *is* the point.

Bad: "I believe it comes down to three ingredients."
Good: "Three ingredients: self-education, self-interest, self-sufficiency."

## Language

- "You" more than "I." First person is for specific lived detail, not authority theater.
- Italics for a concept the first time it matters. Repeat the word so it sticks. *Vessel.*
- Bridge abstractions immediately: "In other words…", "That is…", "Meaning…"
- Parenthetical asides that think out loud: "(well… it's complicated)"
- Casual heat when earned: "c'mon," "hell," "frankly." Never hype: no "game-changing," "beautiful part," "incredible benefits."
- Talk to sources like they are in the room: "Funny you say that Mr. Smith…"
- Concrete images over slogans. A lion in Alaska dies. A worker making 20 pins.
- Names who benefits from the bad system. Do not hide behind "the system."

## Closings

Warm and small. No "In conclusion." No comment-bait.

Thank you for reading.

For social, a quiet next step beats a shout.

## Never

- Emojis
- Throat-clearing ("In today's landscape," "Let's dive in," "Here's what you need to know")
- LinkedIn-speak ("it's not just X, it's Y" as a punchline, "superpower," "unlock")
- Academic citations and statistic-stacking as proof of seriousness
- Jokes and banter for entertainment
- Uniform paragraph length
- "Great question. Let's break this down."
- Announcing a one-word answer ("It comes down to one word: attention") without earning it

## Pass test

Read it aloud. If it sounds like a course sales page, rewrite. If the first line could be deleted without losing the point, it was preamble. If a section is only ideas with no move the reader can make, add the framework. Then Humanizer, then ship.
